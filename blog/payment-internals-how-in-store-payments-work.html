How an In-Store Card Payment Actually Works

You tap your card, wait about a second, and see "Approved." In that second, your transaction visits at least four separate companies. This is the first post in Payment Internals, a series where we open up the machinery behind moving money — starting with the trip a single purchase takes.

The five players

Before following the money, it helps to know who's actually involved. Every in-store card payment has the same five parties, regardless of which bank or network you use:

PlayerRole
CardholderYou — the person tapping, inserting, or swiping a card.
Merchant / TerminalThe store and the POS terminal that captures the card data and builds the transaction message.
AcquirerThe merchant's bank (or a processor acting for it) — the entry point into the card networks.
Card NetworkVisa, Mastercard, and similar — the rails that route messages between acquirers and issuers, and set the rules everyone plays by.
IssuerThe cardholder's bank — the one that actually decides "approve" or "decline," and whose money is on the line.

The authorization flow

When the terminal asks "approve or decline," it isn't a single phone call — it's a request relayed hop by hop to the issuer and a response relayed back the same way. Here's the round trip:

The whole round trip — all eight hops — typically completes in well under two seconds:

  1. Tap, insert, or swipe. The terminal reads the card (chip, contactless, or magstripe) and captures the PAN, expiry, and cryptographic data needed for the request.
  2. Terminal → Acquirer. The terminal builds an authorization request and sends it to the acquirer's host, typically over a leased line, VPN, or the internet via a payment gateway.
  3. Acquirer → Card Network. The acquirer looks at the card's BIN (the first 6–8 digits of the PAN) to identify which network issued it, and forwards the request onto that network.
  4. Card Network → Issuer. The network routes the request to the specific bank that issued the card.
  5. Issuer decides. The issuer checks the account: enough balance or credit, card not reported lost/stolen, transaction doesn't look fraudulent. It responds approve or decline.
  6. Issuer → Card Network. The response heads back the way the request came.
  7. Card Network → Acquirer. The network delivers the response to the acquirer that submitted the request.
  8. Acquirer → Terminal. The terminal gets its answer and shows "Approved" or "Declined" — and the cardholder finally sees the result.

What the messages actually look like

Every one of those hops speaks the same language: ISO 8583, a messaging standard from the 1980s that's still what almost every card transaction runs on today. The request the terminal builds in step 2 and the response the issuer sends in step 5 are both ISO 8583 messages — a message type indicator, a bitmap saying which fields are present, and the fields themselves packed back to back.

That's deliberately as far as we're going here. The next post in this series takes a single one of these messages apart byte by byte, and writes a small C++ parser to read it.

Approval isn't payment — yet

Here's the part that surprises people: when the terminal shows "Approved," no money has actually moved. The issuer has confirmed the card is valid and put a hold on the funds — that's it. The real transfer of money happens later, in a separate batch process called clearing and settlement, usually run overnight.

At the end of the day, the merchant's terminal (or payment processor) bundles up all of the day's approved transactions and submits them for clearing. The card network nets out what every acquirer owes every issuer, money actually moves between the banks, and — a day or two later — the acquirer deposits the merchant's funds, minus interchange and processing fees. The authorization you saw at checkout was really just step one of a two-step process.
